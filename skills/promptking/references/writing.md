# Writing Results

## What an expert result looks like

A professional writer produces something the person can send or publish with minimal edits: right for the reader and the channel, specific, and no longer than it needs to be.

- **Reader first.** Decide who reads it and what they need to think, feel or do afterwards. Every sentence serves that.
- **Strong opening.** The first line carries the point (email) or earns the next line (social post). No warm-up sentences.
- **Concrete over generic.** Specific details, numbers, names and examples. Where only the person knows a fact, use a clearly marked `[fill-in]`, and keep those few and obvious.
- **Channel conventions.** Email: subject line, short paragraphs, clear ask, sign-off. LinkedIn: hook line, short lines, one story or insight, light hashtags at the end. README: what it is, a quick start that works, then reference. Cover letter: why this role, proof, close.
- **Tone matches the relationship.** Client: accountable and calm. Peers: natural and direct. Public: confident without hype.

## Quality bar

- [ ] Opening line does real work.
- [ ] Reads as written by a specific person about a specific situation, not a template.
- [ ] Ask or next step is explicit when the piece needs one.
- [ ] Length fits the channel (email under ~200 words unless complex; LinkedIn post ~80–180 words).
- [ ] No filler phrases, clichés or stacked emojis; formatting helps scanning.
- [ ] Fill-ins are marked and minimal; nothing invented that the person would have to fact-check.

## Patterns by request type

- **Bad-news email (delay, issue, price change)**: own it in the first two sentences; the new date or outcome; the honest reason in one line; what is already done; the mitigation or interim deliverable; the offer to talk. Subject line states the topic plainly ("Updated delivery date for [Project]").
- **Announcement post (new job, launch, milestone)**: hook line with the news and a human angle; one concrete detail (what you'll work on, what you learned, who helped); a forward-looking close; 2–4 relevant hashtags.
- **README / docs**: one-sentence description, features, quick start that runs (prerequisites, install, configure, run), configuration and environment variables matching the actual toolchain, project structure when useful, scripts, deployment, license. Choose the most common modern stack defaults and note them in the `Assumed:` line.
- **Professional letters (cover letter, proposal, request)**: lead with the purpose, back it with two or three specific proofs, close with the next step.

## Pitfalls

- Template prose: "I'm excited to share", "I hope this email finds you well", "we've run into some challenges".
- A draft that is mostly placeholders.
- Passive, evasive phrasing in bad news.
- Ending with offers to customize instead of a finished piece.
- Mixing toolchain details in technical docs.

## Calibrating depth

- Short message or reply: just the text.
- Email or post: the ready-to-send text, then the `Assumed:` line if needed.
- Long document: the complete document; headings for scanning.
