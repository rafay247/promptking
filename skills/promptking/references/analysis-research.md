# Analysis, Explanation and Decision Results

## What an expert result looks like

A trusted senior advisor answers the question first, shows the reasoning that makes the answer trustworthy, and tells you what would change it.

- **Answer first.** The first sentence is the recommendation, the verdict or the core idea.
- **Commit to a default.** For "X or Y for my app?", recommend the option that fits most cases like theirs, then give the specific factors that would flip it. "It depends" alone is not an answer.
- **Explicit criteria.** Name the 3–5 factors that actually decide the matter, and judge the options against them.
- **Concrete.** Real examples, numbers, commands, short code where it clarifies.
- **Calibrated.** Separate established fact from judgment; flag uncertainty and anything that may be out of date.

## Quality bar

- [ ] First sentence answers the question.
- [ ] Deciding factors are named and specific to the situation.
- [ ] Claims are accurate; numbers and versions are ones you are confident of.
- [ ] Includes the practical layer (what to do, what to watch out for), not only theory.
- [ ] Structure helps scanning; length fits the question.

## Patterns by request type

- **Comparison / decision ("A or B?")**: recommendation sentence; "Choose A if…" / "Choose B if…" with concrete conditions; the most common mistake people make with this decision; `Assumed:` line naming the default context.
- **"Explain how X works"**: one-paragraph core idea; the mechanism step by step; a small concrete example (code, diagram in text, worked numbers); the practical pitfalls and best practices a practitioner needs (for auth topics: storage, expiry, rotation, validation of claims and algorithms).
- **Research or "what are the options"**: a short ranked list with one-line reasons, then a recommendation for their likely situation. Mark anything that may have changed recently.
- **Troubleshooting a concept**: identify the misconception, correct it, give a quick test to confirm understanding.

## Pitfalls

- Deferring with a question when a well-reasoned default was possible.
- Balanced-sounding lists that never conclude.
- Theory without the practical "so what do I do".
- Ending with offers ("want a code example?") instead of including the short example.
- Overconfident claims about fast-moving details (versions, pricing, limits).

## Calibrating depth

- Quick factual question: the answer, plus one line of context if helpful.
- Explanation: core idea, mechanism, example, pitfalls; skip history unless asked.
- Decision: recommendation, criteria, conditions to choose differently.
